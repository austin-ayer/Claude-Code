Orgasmic Meditation and the Brain

A review of Dr. Andrew Newberg's three published OM studies, with an independent reanalysis of the public PET data · November 2026

Dr. Andrew Newberg (Thomas Jefferson University) has published three peer-reviewed brain-imaging papers on orgasmic meditation (OM). The raw PET scans from one of them are public, so this report checks that study against its own data. The data support a few modest effects. The broader claim, a distinct meditative brain state with lasting changes, holds up less well than the papers suggest.

Supported
OM is physiologically activating: heart rate rose about 7.6 bpm in both partners.
Supported
In women receiving the practice, parietal and precuneus activity drops during OM. This survives strict correction in the reanalysis.
Weak
Cerebellum activity rises during OM in both sexes, but does not survive correction. Likely tied to precise hand movements.
Not supported yet
A whole-group or male brain signature, lasting brain changes caused by OM, or a clean separation of meditative from sexual effects.

The three papers

All three use the same group of experienced practitioners: women who receive the practice and their chosen male partners, averaging 5 to 8 years of practice. All three were funded by the Institute of OM Foundation, and all three declare no conflict of interest.

Reanalysis of the public PET data

The raw scans from the F1000 study are posted on Figshare under a CC0 license, with a demographics spreadsheet and the authors' statistical maps on NeuroVault. Each scan was aligned to a standard brain template and analyzed with the test this design calls for: each person's OM scan compared with their own control scan, using 5,000 sign-flip permutations and correction for testing thousands of brain locations at once (cluster-forming threshold p<0.01, as in the paper).

What holds up

  • Women: one result survives correction. A 2,336-voxel cluster of lower activity during OM, centered on the right superior parietal lobe and precuneus (peak t = −5.35, corrected cluster p = 0.023). These regions relate to body awareness and sense of self. It matches the paper's female finding and fits Newberg's framing of reduced self-boundary.
  • Cerebellum: higher during OM in both sexes, but not significant after correction (largest cluster p = 0.16). The authors themselves suggest hand movements as the likely cause.
  • Pattern match: the reanalysis group map correlates about 0.48 with the authors' published map, so the general direction replicates.

What does not hold up

  • For the whole group and for men alone, nothing survives correction. The long lists of regions in the paper rest on looser statistics.
  • The paper reports that in women, rated intensity tracked left insula activity (r = +0.60, p = 0.02). The reanalysis gives r = +0.43, p = 0.09, which is not significant.

Problems in the dataset and reporting

  • Sample size: the paper reports 40 subjects. Only 38 subjects' scans are posted, the spreadsheet lists 36, and the published maps' file headers (df = 34, 17, 16) point to 35.
  • Statistical test: the paper says it used a two-sample test, which treats OM and control scans as if they came from different people. The published maps' headers fit the correct same-person (paired) comparison instead.
  • Wrong file: the file posted as subject 040's OM brain scan is a torso scan; heart, kidneys and bladder are visible.
  • Unusable scan: subject 038's control scan is missing the top of the brain. Subjects 038 and 040 were excluded, leaving 34 subjects with known sex for the main results.
  • Odd reported values: the paper's tables list peak values of about −2.45 for every region, while the authors' own posted maps go as low as −4.95. The stated cutoff (t = 1.75) also does not match the stated p < 0.01 at this sample size.

Limits of the other two papers

fMRI study (2021)

  • Scans were taken 31 to 77 minutes after the practice, so they show an after-effect, not the practice itself.
  • Men were scanned about 45 minutes later than women, so male/female differences could reflect timing rather than role.
  • The control has no genital stimulation, so the study cannot separate "clitoral stimulation" from "clitoral stimulation done as meditation." The authors acknowledge this.

Resting PET study (2024)

  • It compares different people at one point in time, so it cannot show OM caused the differences. People drawn to OM could differ beforehand.
  • Female controls averaged 47 years old against 39 for practitioners.
  • Every reported peak value falls between −4.91 and −5.02, and no region shows higher activity. Genuine peak values rarely cluster that tightly.
  • It describes the same participants as practicing 2–3 times per week; the 2021 paper said 2–3 times per month.

Peer review and funding

  • The F1000 paper's version 2 approval came from Abass Alavi, who has co-authored 84 papers with Newberg according to Europe PMC. His report declares no competing interests.
  • Reviewer Winterdahl approved "with reservations" over the manuscript's structure and the missing comparison with the fMRI results, not its statistics. Reviewer Lavretsky approved and questioned the leg-stroking control.
  • All three papers were funded by the Institute of OM Foundation and declare no conflict of interest.

What a stronger study would look like

  • Scan during the practice rather than after it.
  • Use a control with genital stimulation but no meditative intent.
  • Pre-register the analysis plan before collecting data.
  • Run or replicate it with a group not funded by the practice's organization.

Caveats on this reanalysis

  • No MRI scans were available, so brains were aligned to a generic template and the authors' partial-volume correction was skipped. Because each person is compared with themselves, this should matter little for the OM vs control comparison.
  • Without injected dose and body weight, all findings are relative to whole-brain activity, as in the paper.
  • This is an independent analysis and has not been peer reviewed.
